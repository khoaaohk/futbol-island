# IDP research: one plan per child, shared by player, parent and coach

**Date:** 2026-10-09 · **Status:** research input for the IDP redesign · **Not legal advice.** The privacy section is a reading of public regulator texts to frame questions for a qualified lawyer. It is not a legal opinion.

**Scope.** This document adds to [coaches-idp-research-2026-09-19.md](../coaches-idp-research-2026-09-19.md) and does not repeat it. That note covered the product case, the vendor landscape and a pilot plan. This one covers what governing bodies, the research literature, comparable products and children's-privacy law say about an **Individual Development Plan for children aged about 6–12** (7v7/9v9, plus 11v11 and futsal). It assumes Phase 1 has **no accounts and no personal data on the server**: a random save code (three words and a number) holds game progress only, and free-text notes stay on the device. Guardian accounts with verifiable parental consent come in Phase 2.

**How to read the evidence labels**

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | Meta-analysis, large or replicated studies, or binding regulator text read in full |
| **Moderate** | Several smaller or consistent studies, or official governing-body guidance |
| **Limited** | A single study, a small qualitative sample, or a mainly adult/elite sample being applied to children |
| **Practitioner** | Coaching-industry or journalistic source, not peer reviewed |
| *(abstract)* | Only the published abstract was read |
| *(secondary)* | Only another source's summary of it was seen |

## 1. Youth development frameworks: what they say about individual goals before age 12

### 1.1 The FA: Four Corner Model, England DNA, and how often to review

**Four Corner Model.** England Football presents four corners: technical/tactical, physical, psychological and social. It says each is "equally important", that "no one corner works in isolation", and that the model "can be used with all players – regardless of age or ability". It also warns that a child can be advanced in one corner and behind in another ("excellent technique for their age, but … poor decision-making"). The coach's job is "to manage each player in line with their specific needs" ([FA 4 Corner Model](https://learn.englandfootball.com/articles/resources/2022/the-fa-4-corner-model)). The page does **not** prescribe an IDP format or a review cycle. Research use of the model is mostly about academies. One study of professional players' views of it is [Rothwell et al., *J Sports Sci* 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2024.2335583) *(title and search summary only; not read)*.

**England DNA, Foundation Phase (5–11).** The senior and development-team DNA has five elements (who we are, how we play, the future England player, how we coach, how we support). The FA adapts it for 5–11-year-olds into three elements: *the developing child*, *sport, physical activity and the game of football*, and *the relationship between the two*. The key lines are that this "places the child at the centre of everything we do, so that their individual needs are met", and that "meeting the child's needs (and not those of football at this point) is our priority" ([FA: Understanding the core elements of the England DNA](https://www.thefa.com/bootroom/resources/coaching/understanding-the-core-elements-of-the-england-dna)). **Moderate** (official guidance). For an IDP this means the goal belongs to the child's development, not to the team's results.

**"Review every ~6 weeks": where it comes from.** The search found no FA *grassroots* rule that sets a review interval. The six-week figure comes from **elite academy regulation** under the Elite Player Performance Plan. The EFL's *Youth Development Rules 2025/26* require:

- a Multi-disciplinary Review **every 12 weeks for Under-9 to Under-11** and **every six weeks for Under-12 to Under-18** (Rule 45);
- each review to update the player's **Individual Learning Plan**, defined as "measurable objectives for the development that he needs to undertake and the means by which he will obtain those objectives" (definitions; Rule 46);
- meetings with the player **at least four times a season** to "set mutually agreed performance targets" (Rules 47–48);
- meetings with parents at least twice a year, plus an annual written report (Rules 50–52).

The rules' best-practice guidance also suggests benchmarking each player "against that of his peers" ([EFL Youth Development Rules 2025/26, Rules 44–53](https://images.gc.eflservices.co.uk/b6978c10-76a2-11f0-bc93-25ed3b48405b.pdf)). **Strong** as a statement of what academies must do; **Limited** as evidence that these intervals are best for learning. The cadence comes from regulation and was not tested in trials.

> **Takeaway.** Even full-time academies with multi-disciplinary staff review under-11s only quarterly. A volunteer grassroots version should use a light check-in each session or week and a short shared review about every 6 weeks (one school half-term). Peer benchmarking is an elite selection tool; for 6–12-year-olds see §2.6 on why we should not copy it.

### 1.2 US Soccer: grassroots licences and Play-Practice-Play

US Soccer organises grassroots coach education around four game models (**4v4, 7v7, 9v9, 11v11**). The aim is "fun, enjoyable, and developmentally appropriate environments", and the courses teach the **Play-Practice-Play** philosophy ([US Soccer, Grassroots Pathway](https://www.ussoccer.com/stories/2018/01/five-things-to-know-about-the-grassroots-pathway-coaching-initiative)). A member-organisation information packet describes the 4-hour licences as covering "a player-centered approach to coaching", Play-Practice-Play and "the tasks of a grassroots coach" ([US Club Soccer grassroots packet](https://usclubsoccer.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/2023-24-Grassroots-Information-Packet.pdf) *(secondary; search summary)*). Play-Practice-Play opens and closes each session with a game, with a focused practice in between. **Moderate.**

For an IDP, the useful structure is the order: **see it in a game → practise it → try it in a game.** That maps directly onto "watch it on the island → mission → try it at training". The licence materials we could reach do not describe a formal IDP for grassroots players. Treat any "US Soccer IDP template" as unverified.

### 1.3 Canada Soccer and Sport for Life (LTAD/LTPD)

Canada Soccer defines **Grassroots** as "all programming up to Under 13", covering the first three Long-Term Player Development stages. It emphasises "building a love of soccer", belonging, "meaningful feedback to foster a growth mindset", and replacing "ability-based tryouts with interest-driven registration and fluid player pools" ([Canada Soccer, Grassroots](https://canadasoccer.com/play-landing/grassroots-soccer/)). **Moderate.** Sport for Life's current framework names the stages and describes each one ([Sport for Life, Long-Term Development](https://sportforlife.ca/long-term-development/)).

| Stage | Typical ages (Canada Soccer) | What the stage asks for | What a "goal" should look like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Active Start** | U4–U6 | Daily active play; feeling "competent and comfortable" ([stage summary](https://sportforlife.ca/long-term-development/)) | No formal goals. Playful invitations ("Can you stop the ball with your foot?") |
| **FUNdamentals** | U6–U9 boys / U6–U8 girls | "Fun, inclusive, multisport"; mixed structured and unstructured play; agility, balance, coordination, speed ([FUNdamentals](https://sportforlife.ca/stages/fundamentals/)) | One movement or ball-mastery "I can" goal, chosen from pictures; adult helps |
| **Learn to Train** | U9–U12 boys / U8–U11 girls | Foundational skills across sports and positions; 70% training / 30% competition; children benefit from "seeing their own improvement" ([Learn to Train](https://sportforlife.ca/stages/learn-to-train/)) | 1–2 self-chosen process goals with a game situation; first simple reflection |

Age bands are from Canada Soccer's grassroots workshop descriptions as returned by search ([Canada Soccer grassroots coach education](https://canadasoccer.com/news/canada-soccer-launches-online-grassroots-coach-education-program/) *(secondary)*). The stage pages we fetched did not show ages.

### 1.4 Ajax (TIPS) and FC Barcelona (La Masia): handle with care

- **Ajax TIPS** (Technique, Insight, Personality, Speed) is widely reported as Ajax's talent identification and development lens. We found **no official Ajax document and no peer-reviewed description**. The sources are coaching blogs and an FC Barcelona Innovation Hub blog post (25 May 2026). That post also claims Ajax uses "quarterly TIPS evaluations" with measurable objectives ([Barça Innovation Hub blog](https://barcainnovationhub.fcbarcelona.com/blog/the-models-of-youth-teams-in-football/)). **Practitioner / unverified.** One commonly repeated reading is that T and I can be developed while P and S are mostly innate ([SoccerVoice](https://soccervoice.com/2021/09/20/ajax-the-tips-model-their-unique-and-special-approach-to-football/), *practitioner*). That framing would be harmful in a children's IDP, because it labels traits as fixed.
- **La Masia.** FC Barcelona's official *Masia 360* page describes holistic support: academic, emotional education, psychological services, tutoring, and a **"Family and Environment Care Service"** that gives parents tools for supporting children in competition ([FC Barcelona, Masia 360](https://www.fcbarcelona.com/en/club/masia-360)). **Moderate** as a description of what the club says it does. Claims about rondos, positional play and "Barça values" in training come from club blogs and journalism (**practitioner**).
- **Relevance.** Both are elite, selective, full-time academies. What carries over to Futbol Island is the **whole-child scope** and **formal parent support**, not the evaluation machinery.

### 1.5 Aspen Institute Project Play

- **The 8 Plays** (2015 playbook): Ask Kids What They Want; Reintroduce Free Play; Encourage Sport Sampling; Revitalize In-Town Leagues; Think Small; Design for Development; Train All Coaches; Emphasize Prevention ([Project Play report PDF](https://www.aspeninstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Aspen-Institute-Project-Play-Report.pdf)).
- **Why kids quit / what they want.** The report observes that video games give children "lots of action, freedom to experiment, competition without exclusion, … customization, and a measure of control … plus, no parents critiquing their every move". It reports that "6 out of 10 kids say they quit sports because they 'lost interest'", and that kids "want … to try their best" while "dad and mom … still want to talk about the game at dinner". It recommends judging programmes by fun and skills: "Wins and losses shouldn't be the prevailing metric" (same PDF). **Practitioner/policy**, drawing on cited surveys.
- **Parent checklist (ages 6–12 who play).** Ten questions. The ones that bear on an IDP: *Have I asked my child why they want to play?*; *Do I encourage unstructured play?*; *At least two practices for every game*; *Through age 12 … invest in every kid equally*; *Does the program systematically seek feedback from kids and parents?* ([Project Play Parent Checklist 6–12](https://www.aspeninstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/ProjPlayChecklist-6-12.PlayingSports.Interactive_v4.pdf)).
- **Fun research (primary).** Visek et al.'s *FUN MAPS* used concept mapping with 142 youth soccer players, 37 coaches and 57 parents. They identified 81 fun-determinants in 11 dimensions. The top-rated dimensions were **Being a Good Sport, Trying Hard and Positive Coaching**. "Winning" sat inside a lower-rated "Mental Bonuses" dimension ([Visek et al., *J Phys Act Health* 2015, doi:10.1123/jpah.2013-0180](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4201634)). **Moderate** (one large mixed-method study; the paper did not report age differences). Project Play's popular "winning ranked No. 48" is its own reading of this study *(secondary)*.

## 2. The science

### 2.1 Motivation: self-determination theory and autonomy-supportive adults

Ryan and Deci's self-determination theory holds that intrinsic and well-internalised motivation thrive when three needs are met: **autonomy, competence and relatedness** ([Ryan & Deci 2000, *Am Psychol*, doi:10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68)). **Strong** as a theory. In sport, Mageau and Vallerand's model describes coach behaviours that support autonomy (choice within limits, a reason for tasks, acknowledging feelings, opportunities for initiative, non-controlling feedback, avoiding guilt and controlling praise). Together with structure and involvement, these feed the three needs and so athletes' self-determined motivation ([Mageau & Vallerand 2003, *J Sports Sci*, doi:10.1080/0264041031000140374](https://doi.org/10.1080/0264041031000140374) *(abstract; behaviour list from the paper's well-known framework)*). **Moderate.**

On rewards, a meta-analysis of 128 experiments found that **expected tangible rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation**. Engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent rewards gave *d* = −0.40, −0.36 and −0.28. Tangible rewards "tended to be more detrimental for children than college students", while **positive feedback increased** intrinsic motivation (*d* ≈ 0.33) ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, *Psychol Bull*, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627) *(abstract)*). **Strong**, though this literature has been debated (see the comment in the same issue by Lepper et al.).

*Design meaning:* the child chooses the goal (autonomy), the goal is reachable and visible as "I can now…" (competence), and parent and coach are there with them (relatedness). Coins or stickers for *completing the IDP itself* are a risk. Informational feedback is not.

### 2.2 Deliberate play, deliberate practice, and what elite footballers did at 6–12

- **Côté's Developmental Model of Sport Participation (DMSP)** describes *sampling years* (about 6–12) dominated by **deliberate play**: self-led, rule-flexible, intrinsically fun games. *Deliberate practice* (effortful, coach-led, aimed at improvement) increases in adolescence ([Côté, Baker & Abernethy 2007, doi:10.1002/9781118270011.ch8](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781118270011.ch8); [Côté & Vierimaa 2014, doi:10.1016/j.scispo.2014.08.133](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scispo.2014.08.133) *(titles; content from the established model)*). The family's role across these stages is covered in [Côté 1999, doi:10.1123/tsp.13.4.395](https://doi.org/10.1123/tsp.13.4.395) *(not read)*.
- **Ford et al. 2009** compared English elite youth players aged 6–12. Those who went on to professional status by 16 had accumulated **more hours of soccer *play***, but not more practice, competition or other sports, than those who did not. This led to the "early engagement hypothesis" ([*High Ability Studies*, doi:10.1080/13598130902860721](https://doi.org/10.1080/13598130902860721) *(abstract)*). **Limited** (retrospective recall, small elite sample).
- **Ford et al. 2012** studied 328 elite U16 players in seven countries. In childhood they reported about **186 h/yr practice and about 186 h/yr play**, a mix of "early engagement and specialisation" rather than broad multi-sport diversification ([*J Sports Sci*, doi:10.1080/02640414.2012.701762](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2012.701762) *(abstract)*).
- **Hendry, Crocker & Hodges 2014** studied 144 UK academy players and found **no relation** between childhood play hours and later self-determined motivation. Years in the academy system related to *more controlled* motivation in the oldest group ([*J Sports Sci*, doi:10.1080/02640414.2014.880792](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2014.880792) *(abstract)*). Hendry and Hodges later argued for an "early majority engagement" pathway ([*Psychol Sport Exerc* 2018, doi:10.1016/j.psychsport.2018.01.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2018.01.009) *(title only)*).

**Honest reading:** the evidence that play *causes* expertise or motivation is retrospective and mixed. The best-supported conclusions are narrower. Lots of football-specific *play* alongside practice is typical of players who progress, and it does no evident harm. Kids also say playful formats are why they stay (§1.5). Missions should therefore look like **deliberate play**: small games, challenges with self-set rules, and play with friends or siblings. They should not look like drill logs.

### 2.3 Goal-setting for children

**Types.** *Outcome* goals (win, get picked), *performance* goals (a self-referenced standard such as "5 juggles"), and *process* goals (how to execute, such as "look over my shoulder before the ball arrives").

| Evidence | Finding | Strength / caveat |
|---|---|---|
| [Kyllo & Landers 1995, doi:10.1123/jsep.17.2.117](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsep.17.2.117) | 36 studies: goal setting improves sport performance by about 0.34 SD; moderate, absolute, and combined short- plus long-term goals work best | Moderate; mostly adults *(abstract)* |
| [Williamson et al. 2022, doi:10.1080/1750984X.2022.2116723](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2022.2116723) | 27 studies: **process goals d = 1.36**, performance goals d = 0.44, outcome goals d = 0.09; process goals also raised self-efficacy (d = 1.11) | Moderate; small k, few child samples *(abstract)* |
| [Kingston & Hardy 1997, doi:10.1123/tsp.11.3.277](https://doi.org/10.1123/tsp.11.3.277) | Golfers trained in process goals improved self-efficacy, concentration and anxiety control | Limited; adults *(abstract)* |
| [Filby, Maynard & Graydon 1999, doi:10.1080/10413209908404202](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413209908404202) | On a soccer task, **multiple-goal strategies** (outcome + performance + process) beat single types | Limited; n = 40 adults *(abstract)* |
| [Jeong, Healy & McEwan 2021, doi:10.1080/1750984X.2021.1901298](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2021.1901298) | Applied sport goal-setting studies are small, mostly performance-focused, and apply goal-setting theory inconsistently; calls for individualised interventions | Moderate review |
| [Locke & Latham 2002, doi:10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705) | Specific, difficult goals plus commitment and feedback raise performance; work-setting origin | Strong in adults; not child-specific |

**Gap.** We found **no robust trial of goal-setting interventions specifically in 6–12-year-old footballers**. Our recommendation (process goals, one or two at a time) extrapolates from adult and adolescent data plus developmental reasoning: young children focus on one thing at a time and judge themselves from adult feedback (§2.4). Filby's multiple-goal result does not mean we should give children outcome goals. Outcome goals depend on other people (selection, scorelines), and for under-12s they add comparison pressure that the frameworks above advise against.

**"I can" statements (learning intentions and success criteria).** Formative-assessment research in classrooms (the influential review by [Black & Wiliam 1998, doi:10.1080/0969595980050102](https://doi.org/10.1080/0969595980050102), *not read in full*) and Shirley Clarke's practitioner work recommend sharing *what we are learning* and **co-constructing success criteria** with children, focusing on process rather than product ([Clarke, *Unlocking: Learning Intentions and Success Criteria*, Corwin](https://www.corwin.com/books/lisc-271370) *(book listing; practitioner)*). Hattie and Timperley's model says feedback works best when it answers *Where am I going? How am I going? Where to next?* at the task, process or self-regulation level, and **not** at the "self" level (personal praise) ([Hattie & Timperley 2007, doi:10.3102/003465430298487](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487) *(abstract)*). The football version: "**I can** look over my shoulder before the ball comes", with success criteria the child helps write ("I look *before* the pass", "I can say what I saw").

**If–then plans (implementation intentions).** A meta-analysis of 94 tests found that "If situation Y, then I will do X" plans improve goal attainment with ***d* = 0.65** ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1) *(abstract)*). With **school-aged children**, if-then plans (but not plain goal intentions) helped children ignore *attractive* distractions ([Wieber et al. 2011, *Social Psychology*, doi:10.1027/1864-9335/a000041](https://doi.org/10.1027/1864-9335/a000041) *(abstract)*). They also improved response inhibition in children with ADHD ([Gawrilow & Gollwitzer 2007, doi:10.1007/s10608-007-9150-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10608-007-9150-1) *(title only)*). **Moderate** for children in lab tasks; untested in youth football. A natural fit: "**When** my teammate has the ball, **then** I check my shoulder."

### 2.4 Can children rate themselves? Self-assessment by age

- **Under about 8, self-ratings run high.** In Harter's developmental account, young children's self-descriptions are typically very positive and do not separate the wished-for self from the real one. Realistic appraisal grows in middle childhood as social comparison becomes possible ([Harter, *The Construction of the Self*, Guilford](https://www.guilford.com/books/The-Construction-of-the-Self/Susan-Harter/9781462522729) *(secondary; book not read)*). Harter and Pike built a **pictorial** competence scale for 4–7-year-olds ([Harter & Pike 1984, *Child Dev*, doi:10.2307/1129772](https://doi.org/10.2307/1129772)). A later Rasch analysis found its competence subscale had validity problems with 5-year-olds ([Heritage et al. 2020, doi:10.1111/bjep.12268](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12268) *(abstract)*).
- **In sport.** With 134 children aged 8–13, **accuracy of physical-competence judgments rose with age**. Younger children relied more on **adult feedback** and older ones more on **peer comparison** ([Horn & Weiss 1991, *Pediatr Exerc Sci*, doi:10.1123/pes.3.4.310](https://doi.org/10.1123/pes.3.4.310) *(abstract)*). Among 229 young soccer players, children differed in the criteria they used, linked to perceived competence and control ([Horn & Hasbrook 1987, doi:10.1123/jsp.9.3.208](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsp.9.3.208) *(abstract)*).
- **Rating-scale format.** Children aged 5–6 gave **extreme answers** on emotion ratings, and using 3 or 5 options did not change this ([Chambers & Johnston 2002, doi:10.1093/jpepsy/27.1.27](https://doi.org/10.1093/jpepsy/27.1.27) *(abstract)*). With children aged 6–13, **numbered** Likert points agreed poorly with simple yes/no answers, while **word-based** options did better ([Mellor & Moore 2014, doi:10.1093/jpepsy/jst079](https://doi.org/10.1093/jpepsy/jst079) *(abstract)*).
- **Classroom self-assessment.** Ross's review: self-assessment is consistent over short periods, matches teacher judgments only partly, can raise achievement, **inflates without training**, and improves when students are taught *how* to assess ([Ross 2006, *PARE* 11(10)](https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/pare/article/id/1311/) *(search summary; page access blocked)*).

**Implications.** Treat a child's self-check as a **conversation starter, not a measurement**. For under-8s use 3 pictorial options with words ("not yet", "sometimes", "I can!") plus "**not sure yet**", and never chart the answers as a score. For 9–12s, use **evidence prompts** ("Tell me one time you did it") rather than numbers. Pair the child's view with one adult observation. Expect 10–12-year-olds to start comparing themselves with peers on their own, so the product must not supply the comparison.

### 2.5 Feedback and praise: growth mindset, honestly

- **Classic findings.** Fifth-graders praised for intelligence later chose performance goals, persisted less and enjoyed less after failure than those praised for effort ([Mueller & Dweck 1998, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.75.1.33](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.75.1.33) *(abstract)*). With 5–6-year-olds, **person** praise or criticism ("you're a good girl") produced more helpless responses after a setback than **process** feedback ("you found a good way") ([Kamins & Dweck 1999, doi:10.1037/0012-1649.35.3.835](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.35.3.835) *(abstract)*). Parents' process praise at 14–38 months predicted children's incremental beliefs at 7–8 years (N = 53) ([Gunderson et al. 2013, doi:10.1111/cdev.12064](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12064) *(abstract)*).
- **Replication and effect-size debate.**
  - A close replication with 624 Chinese 9–13-year-olds **largely failed** to reproduce Mueller and Dweck's motivation effects ([Li & Bates 2019, doi:10.1037/xge0000669](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000669) *(abstract)*).
  - Meta-analyses found **weak overall** mindset–achievement links and intervention effects, with possible benefits for low-SES or at-risk students ([Sisk et al. 2018, doi:10.1177/0956797617739704](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617739704) *(abstract)*).
  - A bias-corrected review found intervention effects of about *d* = 0.02–0.05, non-significant in the highest-quality studies ([Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023, doi:10.1037/bul0000352](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000352) *(abstract)*).
  - Another meta-analysis found small effects for targeted groups with high-fidelity delivery (academic *d* = 0.14; mental health *d* = 0.32) ([Burnette et al. 2023, doi:10.1037/bul0000368](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000368) *(abstract)*).
  - A pre-registered US national experiment found gains for **lower-achieving adolescents** where **peer norms supported** the message ([Yeager et al. 2019, *Nature*, doi:10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y) *(abstract)*).
- **What survives.** "Mindset" as a lesson to teach children has **small and contested** effects. The narrower, well-supported practices are: feedback about **task, process and strategy** (Hattie & Timperley 2007); informational positive feedback (Deci et al. 1999); a **mastery-involving climate** ("do your best, learn, mistakes are part of it"). A coach intervention built on that climate reduced young basketball players' performance anxiety over a season ([Smith, Smoll & Cumming 2007, doi:10.1123/jsep.29.1.39](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsep.29.1.39) *(abstract)*). **Moderate.**
- **Practitioner heuristics (not peer reviewed).** The Positive Coaching Alliance teaches **ELM** (Effort, Learning, Mistakes are OK) and suggests about **5 specific, truthful praises for each constructive criticism**. It also advises parents to "avoid an immediate and critical debrief right after the game" ([PCA parent/guardian letter](https://positivecoach.org/wp-content/uploads/Parent-Guardian-Letter_Attachment.pdf)). **Practitioner.** The 5:1 ratio is a heuristic, not a validated threshold.

**Copy rules for the product:** praise the *strategy or effort tied to the goal* ("You checked your shoulder twice before receiving"). Never praise the trait ("You're a natural"). Never use "not good enough yet" framings that imply a fixed rank.

### 2.6 Relative age, maturation, and why we never rank children

- **Relative age effect (RAE).** National youth squads across ten European countries over-represent players born early in the selection year, because older-in-year children have physical advantages and get labelled "talented" ([Helsen, Van Winckel & Williams 2005, doi:10.1080/02640410400021310](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640410400021310) *(abstract)*). A meta-analysis of 38 studies and 253 samples found RAEs **consistent but small**, growing with competitive level and adolescence ([Cobley et al. 2009, doi:10.2165/00007256-200939030-00005](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200939030-00005) *(abstract)*). **Strong.**
- **RAE reaches the grassroots through dropout.** Among 363,590 French male players who dropped out, late-born players were over-represented **from U-9 to U-18**, but not at U-7 ([Delorme, Boiché & Raspaud 2010, doi:10.1080/02640411003663276](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640411003663276) *(abstract)*).
- **Maturation.** Biological maturity strongly affects size and functional capacity in youth football ([Malina et al. 2004, doi:10.1007/s00421-003-0995-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-003-0995-z) *(title)*). **Bio-banding** groups players by maturity instead of birth year ([Cumming et al. 2017, doi:10.1519/SSC.0000000000000281](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000281) *(abstract)*). In a Premier League bio-banded tournament, late maturers valued the chance to show their skills ([Cumming et al. 2018, doi:10.1080/02640414.2017.1340656](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2017.1340656) *(abstract)*). Most bio-banding evidence covers ages 11–14 in academies.

**Meaning for us:** any cross-child comparison inside a birth-year cohort (leaderboards, "top 10% for passing", ability bands) partly measures **birth month and puberty timing**, not learning. Comparing a child only with their own earlier self avoids this bias.

**Patterns worth copying:** child-authored evidence with adult moderation (ClassDojo/Seesaw); **co-agreed progress** (360Player); the adult view kept behind a gate and away from the play loop (KA Kids/Duolingo ABC); strict "your child only" visibility.

**Pitfalls, with evidence**

- **Manipulative engagement design is common in children's apps.** In apps used by 160 children aged 3–5, **80%** had at least one manipulative feature. Common ones were "attractive lures" (rewards to keep playing, 45%), navigation constraints (46%), parasocial pressure from characters (25%), and "come back tomorrow" daily-reward hooks. Prevalence was higher in lower-education households ([Radesky et al. 2022, *JAMA Netw Open*, doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.17641](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/)). **Moderate.**
- **Streaks work through the fear of breaking them.** Across seven studies (adults), **intact** logged streaks increased later engagement compared with **broken** ones. People treat keeping the streak as a goal in itself, and the drop is larger when they blame themselves for the break ([Silverman & Barasch 2022, *J Consumer Res*, doi:10.1093/jcr/ucac029](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029) *(abstract)*). Duolingo's own blog says streaks harness **loss aversion** ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit) *(vendor)*). We found **no peer-reviewed study of streaks in under-12s**. Given SDT and the reward meta-analysis (§2.1), a streak on an IDP would turn "practise because I want to improve" into "practise so I don't lose my streak".
- **Regulator view.** The ICO says that "designing in data-driven features which make it difficult for children to disengage … is likely to breach the … fairness principle". It tells services to present options to continue **neutrally**, to avoid features that "automatically extend use", and to offer pause "without losing their progress" ([ICO Standard 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)). Its games guidance recommends checkpoints, automatic saving, break prompts, and assessing time-limited offers ([ICO top tips for games designers](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/top-tips-for-games-designers-how-to-comply-with-the-childrens-code/)).
- **Surveillance-style parent dashboards** (minutes played, every answer, real-time alerts) match the "controlling" end of Holt et al.'s continuum. They also trigger the ICO's parental-controls standard, which requires telling the child (§4.3).
- **Comparison and leaderboards** reintroduce relative-age and maturation bias (§2.6) and peer comparison at exactly the age children begin to rely on it (§2.4).

### 4.2 Are save codes "personal information"?

**The case that a save code is a persistent identifier.** It lets us "recognize a user over time". A literal reading of definition (7) also requires "**across** different websites or online services", and a code typed only into Futbol Island does not do that. However, the definition's examples (IP address, device ID) show the FTC reads it broadly. Server requests also expose the **IP address** in transit, which is itself listed. The repo's design keeps no IP and stores only an HMAC of the code ([save-codes.md](../save-codes.md)), but receiving the IP to respond is "network communications", one of the internal operations.

**The harder question is definition (11).** If the code *is* a persistent identifier, then progress data "combined with" it could become personal information. Exception (c)(7) only covers collecting "a persistent identifier **and no other personal information**". Two readings follow:

- **(A)** Allowlisted, non-free-text game progress (lessons done, coins, chosen focus id) is not information "concerning the child" in the §312.2 sense, or is merely the content personalised under internal operations. In that case (c)(7) covers it, with a §312.4(d)(3) notice.
- **(B)** Progress that reflects the child's football goals and self-ratings *is* information concerning the child. Combined with the code it becomes PI, so (c)(7) does not cover it and parental consent would be needed.

The design choices that strengthen reading (A):

- keep IDP **reflections, self-checks and free text on the device**, never synced;
- sync only **ids from a fixed list** (e.g. `goal: scan-before-receiving`);
- never let the code double as a username or contact handle;
- use it for nothing except restoring the island;
- publish the internal-operations notice and the retention policy.

**[LAWYER]** must decide between (A) and (B).

**UK/EU angle.** Under UK GDPR, pseudonymous data that can be linked to an individual is still personal data. The ICO's code applies to services "likely to be accessed by children" ([ICO code standards](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/code-standards/)). Assume a save-code record is personal data in the UK, and apply minimisation, retention, a DPIA and transparency to it.

### 4.4 GDPR Article 8 (age of digital consent)

Where an online service relies on **consent**, EU GDPR Art. 8 sets 16 as the age of digital consent, which member states may lower to no less than 13. The UK's version reads "at least **13 years old**" ([UK GDPR Art. 8 on legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/8)). Below that age, a parent must consent. Many EU states use 13–16 (national ages not verified here). Avoiding consent-based processing in Phase 1 (an alternative lawful basis plus minimisation) is a **[LAWYER]** question.

### 4.5 Connecting parents and coaches without personal data

| Mechanism | How | Personal data? | Status |

The child scans it and the plan appears locally | No child data in the code. If it resolves to our URL, the scanner's IP reaches the server (internal ops) | Low risk |

| **Save code read out to parent** | Parent types the code on their phone to see the same island | Same as §4.2; the parent then sees everything synced | Medium; the child must be told (Std 11) |

## 5. Top 10 insights for the Futbol Island IDP

1. The FA's Foundation Phase puts "the child at the centre … meeting the child's needs (and not those of football at this point)". The plan is the child's, not the team's. [FA England DNA](https://www.thefa.com/bootroom/resources/coaching/understanding-the-core-elements-of-the-england-dna)
2. Even elite academies review under-11 plans only every 12 weeks (6 weeks from U12) and agree targets *with* the player, so a light weekly check-in plus a roughly 6-week review is generous, not lax. [EFL Youth Development Rules 2025/26](https://images.gc.eflservices.co.uk/b6978c10-76a2-11f0-bc93-25ed3b48405b.pdf)
3. Process goals produce the largest performance effects (d ≈ 1.36 vs 0.09 for outcome goals) and raise self-efficacy, though child-specific trials are scarce. [Williamson et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2022.2116723)
4. "When X, then I'll do Y" plans have a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment and work for school-aged children in lab tasks. [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1)
5. Children judge their competence more accurately as they age; younger ones lean on adult feedback, older ones on peer comparison, so self-ratings are conversation starters, not data. [Horn & Weiss 1991](https://doi.org/10.1123/pes.3.4.310)
6. Young children answer emotion rating scales at the extremes, and numbered Likert points confuse 6–13-year-olds, so use few, worded, pictorial options. [Mellor & Moore 2014](https://doi.org/10.1093/jpepsy/jst079)
7. Process feedback protects 5–6-year-olds after setbacks better than person praise, while generic "growth mindset" interventions show small, contested effects. [Kamins & Dweck 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.35.3.835) / [Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000352)
8. Parent-created motivational climate predicts children's self-esteem, anxiety and autonomous motivation over and above the coach's climate, so the parent view is where much of the plan's effect sits. [O'Rourke et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2014.907838)
9. Children want parents to comment on effort and attitude, not technique, and to share goals with them; the post-game car ride is where this most often goes wrong. [Knight, Boden & Holt 2010](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2010.495324) / [Knight & Holt 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2013.10.010)
10. Comparing children within a birth year partly measures birth month and maturity, and late-born players drop out more from U-9 upward, so rankings are biased as well as demotivating. [Delorme, Boiché & Raspaud 2010](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640411003663276)

## 6. Implications for design

Each rule links back to the evidence above. Where evidence is thin, the rule is labelled **(hypothesis)**.

**Goals**
- **Process goals only** for 6–12 (e.g. "check my shoulder before the ball arrives"). No outcome goals (wins, selection, goals scored). Performance goals only as self-referenced "I can" milestones for 9–12. *(§2.3)*
- **One active goal for under-8s, at most two for 9–12.** *(Developmental reasoning plus Williamson; hypothesis on the exact number.)*
- **Co-created choice.** The child picks from 3 options the coach or parent pre-selects, all phrased as "I can…". Show the reason ("Why it helps") in one line. *(SDT §2.1; Clarke §2.3)*
- **Add one if–then plan** ("When my teammate has the ball, then I…") for 8+. For under-8s the adult says it aloud. *(§2.3)*
- **Whole-child options.** Each pack offers at least one non-technical goal (social: "I say well done to a teammate"; psychological: "After a mistake I do my reset breath"). *(Four Corners §1.1)*

**Missions and practice**
- **Deliberate-play missions.** Missions are small games, self-set challenges, and play with a sibling or friend, not drill logs. Follow Play-Practice-Play: watch it on the island → mini-game → try it at training. *(§1.2, §2.2)*
- **No completion rewards for the IDP itself.** Use informational feedback ("You found 3 times you scanned"), not coins for filling the plan. Coins stay in the game economy. *(Deci et al. 1999)*

**Self-check and review**
- **Under-8s: 3-point pictorial self-check plus "not sure yet",** with words, no numbers, never charted. For 9–12, an evidence prompt ("Tell one time you did it") plus optional words; still no numbers. *(§2.4)*
- **Review about every 6 weeks** (half-term), with a light check-in after sessions. Under-8s may need shorter loops of 3–4 weeks **(hypothesis)**. At review, choose **keep going / tweak / new goal**. Never "pass/fail". *(§1.1)*
- **Pair the child's view with one adult observation,** written as task/process feedback ("I saw you look before receiving twice"). *(Hattie & Timperley; Horn & Weiss)*

**What we never show**
- **No rankings, scores, percentages, leaderboards or peer comparison,** and no "talent" or position labels. Progress is shown only against the child's own earlier self. *(§2.6; ICO Std 12)*
- **No streaks, no "come back tomorrow" hooks, no time pressure.** Pausing or skipping a week loses nothing, and continue prompts are neutral. *(§3; ICO Std 5/13)*

**Feedback copy**
- **Praise effort and strategy, not traits.** Copy bank: "You tried the new way", "You kept looking even after a mistake". Banned: "natural", "talented", "best on the team". *(§2.5)*

**Parents and coaches**
- **Parent role = ask, listen, encourage.** The parent card offers one question ("What did you notice when you checked your shoulder?"), one encouragement, and **"I love to watch you play"** as the suggested closer *(practitioner)*. No technical coaching prompts and no parent rating of the child. *(Knight et al.; Holt et al.; PCA)*
- **Car-ride-home guidance:** "Let them start the conversation; wait before reviewing; talk about effort and fun first; save the plan for later in the week." *(Elliott & Drummond; Tamminen et al. 2017; PCA)*
- **Share goals across the triangle.** The same one-line goal appears on the child, parent and coach cards, so all three describe it the same way. *(Knight & Holt 2014: "share and communicate goals")*
- **Coach as observer, not scorer.** The coach card asks for one dated sighting and one next cue. Templates by format (7v7/9v9/11v11/futsal) reuse the same goal ids. *(earlier note; §1.2)*

**Privacy and transparency**
- **Phase 1 privacy pattern:** reflections, self-checks and notes stay **on the device**. Coaches connect via goal-id QR codes and printed or on-screen cards. Parents connect via the device's own share sheet or by looking at the plan together. Any email we send waits on the [LAWYER] answers. *(§4.5)*
- **Tell the child who sees what.** Every IDP screen carries a child-readable "Only on this device" or "Your grown-up can see this" badge, with an obvious sign when a parent view is active. *(ICO Std 4, 11)*
- **Parent education is part of the product.** Add short, stage-specific tips (5–8 vs 9–12) inside the parent card, since online parent education has early positive signals. *(Thrower et al. 2018; Dorsch et al. 2017)*
